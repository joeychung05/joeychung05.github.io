narrative

I'm a Chemistry undergraduate at the University of Michigan, headed toward process and materials work in semiconductor manufacturing. Almost everything I've done sits at a surface — plating bismuth telluride with electroless nickel, soldering leads onto finished modules, measuring how thick a film actually came out. A process only counts once it gives the same result twice.

I didn't start in chemistry. I started in Food Science at UW–Madison, where the first two years were general science anyway: organic and general chemistry, physics, biology. Then I spent nine months at DAEYANG Co., Ltd. on thermoelectric module production. What stayed with me was the surface itself — whether a wedge was clean and evenly plated, and what happened downstream when it wasn't. I already had the chemistry coursework behind me, so I transferred to Michigan for the broader degree, chemistry rather than food science, and pointed it at materials.

That factory floor also taught me how failure travels. An inconsistent cleaning step doesn't announce itself; it shows up as a dead module weeks later. I proposed a titanium holding jig to close one of those failure modes and drew the specifications for it. The build was deferred against module priorities, but the habit of tracing a defect back to the step that caused it stayed.

Before any of it: 18 months in the auxiliary police in mandatory national service in Pohang, South Korea, 7 of them leading a 12-officer squad. It taught me what a procedure is actually for, which is the moment conditions change faster than the plan.

Now I'm in the Maldonado Group at Michigan on thin film metrology — measuring layer thickness and reading formation patterns by AFM and ellipsometry, with ALD training underway. Same questions, better instruments.
