Layer 02

DAEYANG Co., Ltd.

Research Assistant · Thermoelectric module R&D
2024.09 — 2025.05
9 months
Daegu, South Korea

DAEYANG is a corporate R&D institute developing thermoelectric materials and modules for energy and environmental applications. I worked across most of the line, which turned out to be useful: a wedge that plates unevenly looks like nothing at the bath, but like a module that reads wrong three weeks later.

The line runs like this: Bi, Te, and Sb feedstock is ground, mixed by ball milling, and consolidated under heat and pressure into a cylindrical pellet. The pellet is sliced into coins, then cut into quarters or eighths, called sector wedges. Wedges are degreased, plated, diced into cubes, and cleaned again. Only then are they legs, ready for SMT.

Part 01

Surface prep & plating

My main stretch was surface preparation and the plating bath. Before plating, wedges were degreased, mainly by sonication in IPA, so residue from cutting and handling didn't end up under the coating; the diced legs went through the same cleaning afterward.

Plating was medium-phosphorus (6–8% P) electroless nickel on bismuth telluride, with pH monitoring and solution adjustment through each run and samples handled in process fixtures. A drifting bath doesn't fail loudly; it leaves an uneven coating on a wedge that becomes many legs. One uneven wedge isn't one bad part. It's every leg that comes out of it.

How the wedges were held through the bath was the related problem, so I proposed a custom titanium holding jig on my own initiative, drawings and specifications included, and brought it to a senior researcher. Scheduling, fabricator sourcing, and minimum order quantities stalled the build, but specifying a fixture against a named failure mode is the part I kept.

Part 02

Inspection & incoming QC

After dicing I ran dimensional inspection and defect classification on the legs: critical dimensions against design tolerances under digital microscopy, sorted into defect types (machining error, burring, asymmetry).

Not all the legs were ours, either. DAEYANG runs joint national R&D with the Korea Electrotechnology Research Institute (KERI), and on one commissioned build using KERI's own legs, I separately ran incoming inspection and tray loading. Whatever the source, nonconforming legs came out before SMT: one bad leg in a 128-leg module is a bad module.

Part 03

Module assembly & test

On the assembly side I supported senior engineers building 8×8 modules, 128 legs each: solder paste prep, substrate mounting frame setup, automated paste printing and leg placement, soldering positive and negative lead wires to electrodes after reflow, and sourcing lead-free, high-temperature paste. Weekly output ran 20 to 30 modules, scaling past 50 during government R&D cycles.

At the end of the line I ran final electrical inspection: resistance and voltage against spec, results documented in Excel for traceability, nonconforming units flagged for retest. I also collected generator performance data under senior researcher guidance across five cooling configurations: forced and natural air, forced and natural water, and antifreeze based.

Layer 01

Republic of Korea Auxiliary Police

Sergeant · Mandatory national service
2019.11 — 2021.05
18 months
South Korea

Military service is compulsory in South Korea. I served mine in the auxiliary police in Pohang, under the Ministry of National Defense and the Korean National Police Agency, and finished as a sergeant.

For 7 months of the 18 I led a 12-officer squad through high-pressure field deployments: protest response, and missing person searches across mountainous and coastal terrain. We located 3 missing individuals and escorted them to safety, which earned an official commendation.

The rest was public safety work: police lines during civil disturbances, joint readiness drills with the ROK Marine Corps including chemical threat response protocols, driving support for personnel and equipment, and station entry control, guiding civilians to the correct department rather than processing their reports myself.

What it left me with is narrower than it sounds. A procedure written in advance is worth very little; a squad that has rehearsed one is worth a great deal. That distinction is most of what I think about when I read a process document now.
